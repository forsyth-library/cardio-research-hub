    Check a source

    Found a website on your own? Remember: Googling ain’t it. Before you use a source, check it with these four steps. Fact-checkers call them SIFT. It takes a few minutes.

    1. S

      Stop

      Before you read it, ask: do I know what this is? Pick the closest match.

      What kind of source is it?

    2. I

      Investigate who made it

      Open a new tab and search the name of the website or author. Who are they? Are they experts? Are they trying to sell you something?

    3. F

      Find what other sources say

      Search the main idea, not the website. Do trusted sources (NIH, CDC, the American Heart Association) say the same thing?

      Do trusted sources agree?
    4. T

      Trace it back to the study

      News stories and websites often talk about a study. Click through to the study itself and check that it really says that.

      • Was it published in a science journal? (That usually means it was peer-reviewed: checked by other scientists.)
      • Who was studied: people, animals, or cells? How many?
      • Did it show that one thing causes another, or only that they happen together?
    Your decision

    Watch out for

    • Sites that sell a product, supplement, or treatment they write about.
    • “.org” sites. Anyone can buy one, so .org doesn't mean you can trust it.
    • No author, no date, or no references.
    • Big promises like “cures” or “proves.” One study can’t prove that much.

    Cite a source

    Make a citation for your References, in APA style. For a study, paste its DOI or PubMed number and the form fills itself in. For a website, fill in the form. Then copy it, or save it to My References.

    A DOI is the study’s ID number. It starts with 10. and is usually near the top of the study. A PubMed or PMC number, or a link to the study, works too. For websites, fill in the form below or try MyBib ↗.

    One per line: Last name, First name. If a group wrote it (like the CDC), type the group’s full name.

    In APA, only the first word, names, and the first word after a colon get capital letters.

    Example

    Keeps the italics when you paste into Google Docs or Word. Always double-check it.

    What an APA citation looks like Example

    Study (journal article)

    Bernardi, L., Porta, C., & Sleight, P. (2006). Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and respiratory changes induced by different types of music in musicians and non-musicians: The importance of silence. Heart, 92(4), 445–452. https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600

    Website from a group like the NIH

    National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. (n.d.). How the heart works. https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart

    No date on the page? Use (n.d.), which means “no date.” When a group wrote the page, don't list its name twice.

    My References

    The sources for the References part of your PBAT. For each one, finish the citation and write two short notes: what it says, and how it helps your PBAT. Then copy the list into your Google Doc.

    Copy your list before you leave

    Your sources are saved only in this browser, on this computer. They won't be here on a different computer, and school computers may erase them. Before you leave the site, click Copy my References and paste it into your Google Doc.

    Show me an example Example

    Bernardi, L., Porta, C., & Sleight, P. (2006). Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and respiratory changes induced by different types of music in musicians and non-musicians: The importance of silence. Heart, 92(4), 445–452.

    What it says: 24 adults listened to six kinds of music. Faster music raised their breathing rate, blood pressure, and heart rate. A two-minute pause of silence lowered all three, even below where they started.

    How it helps: This backs up the “because” in my hypothesis: fast music speeds up the heart. It also shows how scientists measured heart rate before and during music, which helps me plan my experiment.

    Search for studies

    Need more than what’s on this site? Googling ain’t it. Search a science database instead. Databases don’t understand questions; they match words. So build your search from a few key words.

    Build your search

    1. Key words we spotted. Click one to use it:
    2. Pick 2–3 key words, and add other words that mean the same thing

      Each article you get will be about every key word. Scientists sometimes use different words, like pulse for “heart rate” or adolescents for “teenagers,” so add those too.

    3. Your search

      How the search works

      • AND means every key word must be in the article. You get fewer, closer results.
      • OR means any of these words can be in the article. You get more results.
      • Quotation marks keep words together, like "heart rate".

      Too many results? Add another key word. Too few? Take one out, or add more words that mean the same thing.

    Or search quickly

    1. Check it before you use it.

    Databases to try

    Search tips

    • Search key words, not sentences. For “How does music affect heart rate in teens?”, search music heart rate adolescents.
    • Too many results? Add a word (adolescents, exercise) or use a more exact word.
    • Too few? Take a word out, or try the word scientists use: respiratory rate for “breathing rate,” vital capacity for “lung capacity,” hypertension for “high blood pressure.”
    • Can't open the whole study? Ask the library before you give up on it.

    Topic map

    Pick a topic. Each branch is one part of it. In each branch, write questions you wonder about. Start them with How or Why.

    Show me an example branch Model

    Branch: Sleep (topic: Lifestyle & the heart)

    Why does blood pressure usually drop at night?
    How does staying up late on phones change teens' heart rate?
    Why would short sleep raise blood pressure over time?

    Research question

    A strong research question can't be answered by one fact. It asks how or why, and you can find evidence for it.

    ProblemWeakStronger
    Too broadWhat is heart disease?How does LDL oxidation contribute to plaque formation in coronary arteries?
    Yes/noIs exercise good for your heart?How does aerobic training change stroke volume, and why?
    Can't be researchedWill heart disease be cured by 2040?What evidence suggests gene therapy could treat inherited cardiomyopathies?
    Opinion onlyShould fast food be banned?How have menu-labeling or sodium policies affected blood pressure in U.S. adults?

    Quick check

      This is a computer's guess. Your teacher has the final say.

      Sub-questions

      What smaller questions do you need to answer first? Aim for 2–4. These become the rows of your evidence matrix.

        Show me an example Model

        Research question: How does short sleep affect blood pressure in teenagers?

        1. How does the body normally regulate blood pressure during sleep?
        2. What changes in the body when teens get less sleep?
        3. What do studies of teens show about sleep length and blood pressure?

        Evidence matrix

        Rows are your sub-questions; columns are your sources from My sources. In each box, note what that source says about that sub-question. A row with fewer than 2 sources needs more research. Scroll sideways to see all your sources.

        Taking good notes

        • Use your own words. Write the idea, not the sentence.
        • Exact words? Put them in “quotation marks” and add the page or paragraph, like (p. 4) or (para. 7).
        • Write down numbers with their units and who was studied, like “238 teens, 3.5× higher odds.”

        Claims, evidence, and reasoning

        For each sub-question, answer it in one sentence (claim), choose the notes that prove it (evidence), and explain the biology that connects them (reasoning).

        Show me an example Model

        Claim: Teens who sleep less than about 6.5 hours a night are more likely to have high blood pressure.

        Evidence: In a study of 238 adolescents, short or poor sleep was linked to about 3.5 times higher odds of elevated blood pressure, even after accounting for weight (Javaheri et al., 2008).

        Reasoning: Blood pressure normally dips during sleep because sympathetic nervous system activity falls. With less sleep, the body spends more hours in that higher-pressure state, and over time this strains artery walls.
