    Evaluate a source

    Found something on your own? Before you trust it, check it the way fact-checkers do, with the four SIFT moves. It takes a few minutes per source.

    1. S

      Stop

      Before you read it closely, ask: do I know what this is? Pick the closest match.

      What kind of source is it?

    2. I

      Investigate the source

      Leave the page. Open a new tab and search for the publisher or author. Who are they? Are they experts in this? Who pays for them, and would they gain from you believing this?

    3. F

      Find better coverage

      Search the main claim, not the source. Do trusted sources (NIH, CDC, the American Heart Association, research reviews) say the same thing?

      Do trusted sources agree?
    4. T

      Trace claims to the original

      News stories and websites often describe a study. Follow the link to the original research and check that the claim matches what the study actually found.

      • Was the study peer-reviewed (published in a journal)?
      • Who or what was studied: people, animals, or cells? How many?
      • Did it show that one thing causes another, or only that they happen together?
    Your decision

    Watch out for

    • Sites that sell a product, supplement, or treatment they write about.
    • “.org” sites. Anyone can buy one; it doesn't mean nonprofit or trustworthy.
    • No author, no date, or no references.
    • Claims that sound too strong (“cures,” “proves”) for what one study can show.

    Cite a source

    Build an APA 7 citation. Look up an article by its DOI or PubMed ID, or fill in the form. Then copy it, or save it to My sources.

    Paste a DOI, a PubMed ID (PMID), a PMC ID, or a link to the article on PubMed or a journal's site. For web pages, fill in the form below by hand or try MyBib ↗.

    One per line as Last, First Middle. For an organization, type its full name.

    APA uses sentence case: capitalize only the first word, proper nouns, and the word after a colon.

    Example

    Pastes with italics into Google Docs and Word. Always double-check against the APA style guide.

    What an APA citation looks like Model

    Journal article

    Libby, P., Ridker, P. M., & Maseri, A. (2002). Inflammation and atherosclerosis. Circulation, 105(9), 1135–1143. https://doi.org/10.1161/hc0902.104353

    Web page from an organization

    National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. (n.d.). How the heart works. https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart

    No date on the page? Use (n.d.). When the organization is the author, don't repeat its name as the website.

    My sources

    Your working bibliography. For each source, complete the citation and write a two-part annotation: what it says, and how it helps your research. Then copy it into your Google Doc.

    Show me an example annotation Model

    Javaheri, S., Storfer-Isser, A., Rosen, C. L., & Redline, S. (2008). Sleep quality and elevated blood pressure in adolescents. Circulation, 118(10), 1034–1040.

    What it says: In a study of 238 teens, those who slept poorly or less than 6.5 hours were about 3.5 times more likely to have high blood pressure, even after accounting for weight.

    How it helps: This is direct evidence that sleep affects teens' blood pressure. It's a real study of teenagers, not adults, and it gives a number I can use.

    Find more sources

    Need research studies beyond the collection? Databases don't understand questions. They match words. Build a search, run it, and save what you find.

    Build your search

    1. Key ideas we spotted. Click one to use it:
    2. Pick 2–3 key ideas, and add other words for each

      Every article must be about each key idea. Other words catch articles that use different vocabulary, like hypertension for “high blood pressure.”

    3. Your search

      How the search works

      • AND joins key ideas. Every idea must appear, so it narrows results.
      • OR joins words for the same idea. Any of them can appear, so it widens results.
      • Quotation marks keep a phrase together.

      Too many results? Add another key idea. Too few? Remove an idea, or add more other words.

    Databases to try

    Search tips

    • Search key terms, not sentences. For “How does sleep affect blood pressure in teens?”, search sleep blood pressure adolescents.
    • Too many results? Add a term (adolescents, exercise) or choose a more specific word.
    • Too few? Remove a term, or try a synonym: hypertension for “high blood pressure,” myocardial infarction for “heart attack.”
    • Can't get the full article? Ask the library before you give up on it.

    Topic map

    Pick a topic. Each branch is one part of it. In each branch, write questions you wonder about. Start them with How or Why.

    Show me an example branch Model

    Branch: Sleep (topic: Lifestyle & the heart)

    Why does blood pressure usually drop at night?
    How does staying up late on phones change teens' heart rate?
    Why would short sleep raise blood pressure over time?

    Research question

    A strong research question can't be answered by one fact. It asks how or why, and you can find evidence for it.

    ProblemWeakStronger
    Too broadWhat is heart disease?How does LDL oxidation contribute to plaque formation in coronary arteries?
    Yes/noIs exercise good for your heart?How does aerobic training change stroke volume, and why?
    Can't be researchedWill heart disease be cured by 2040?What evidence suggests gene therapy could treat inherited cardiomyopathies?
    Opinion onlyShould fast food be banned?How have menu-labeling or sodium policies affected blood pressure in U.S. adults?

    Quick check

      This is a computer's guess. Your teacher has the final say.

      Sub-questions

      What smaller questions do you need to answer first? Aim for 2–4. These become the rows of your evidence matrix.

        Show me an example Model

        Research question: How does short sleep affect blood pressure in teenagers?

        1. How does the body normally regulate blood pressure during sleep?
        2. What changes in the body when teens get less sleep?
        3. What do studies of teens show about sleep length and blood pressure?

        Evidence matrix

        Rows are your sub-questions; columns are your sources from My sources. In each box, note what that source says about that sub-question. A row with fewer than 2 sources needs more research. Scroll sideways to see all your sources.

        Taking good notes

        • Use your own words. Write the idea, not the sentence.
        • Exact words? Put them in “quotation marks” and add the page or paragraph, like (p. 4) or (para. 7).
        • Write down numbers with their units and who was studied, like “238 teens, 3.5× higher odds.”

        Claims, evidence, and reasoning

        For each sub-question, answer it in one sentence (claim), choose the notes that prove it (evidence), and explain the biology that connects them (reasoning).

        Show me an example Model

        Claim: Teens who sleep less than about 6.5 hours a night are more likely to have high blood pressure.

        Evidence: In a study of 238 adolescents, short or poor sleep was linked to about 3.5 times higher odds of elevated blood pressure, even after accounting for weight (Javaheri et al., 2008).

        Reasoning: Blood pressure normally dips during sleep because sympathetic nervous system activity falls. With less sleep, the body spends more hours in that higher-pressure state, and over time this strains artery walls.
